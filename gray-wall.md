# The Gray Wall of Singularity

**Author:** Igor Zhuk ([igor.m.zhuk@gmail.com](mailto:igor.m.zhuk@gmail.com))  
*Preprint / Research Project DOT (Distinction Observable Theory)*  
*Project Archive:* [github.com/Nondual-Observer/DOTheory](https://github.com/Nondual-Observer/DOTheory)  
*Russian Edition:* [ru/gray-wall.md](ru/gray-wall.md) (GitHub) &bull; [Веб-версия (RU)](https://nondual-observer.github.io/ObserverInterfece/ru/gray-wall.html)

> **Epistemic status:** _Exploratory / Scenario Modeling. I estimate the probability of the AGI trajectory following an Intelligible Theory (compact and comprehensible to humans), rather than remaining permanently an opaque black box, to be moderate (~15–25%), yet consider it critically underexplored. The focus of this essay is a thought experiment with a single strictly defined boundary condition._

---

The Technological Singularity is typically described as the moment beyond which human comprehension fails to keep pace with machine intelligence. Artificial intelligence will discover new laws, invent technologies, and refine its own models at such a velocity that humans will merely consume the fruits of its labor.

Even today, most everyday devices appear nearly magical to anyone unfamiliar with their inner workings. Yet inside this "magic" lie principles, research, calculations, and engineering solutions that are fundamentally intelligible to human beings. At the same time, no single person holds all knowledge in their head. Knowledge exists as the collective heritage of countless scientists and specialists—transmitted, verified, and developed collaboratively. Long before the era of AGI, science has always been humanity's collective mode of understanding the world.

**What if the primary breakthrough of AGI becomes part of this shared repository of knowledge in exactly the same way?**

Imagine that artificial intelligence discovers a mathematical theory that is sufficiently intelligible to humans, yet profoundly deep. A theory through which previously disparate branches of mathematics are revealed as facets of a single underlying structure. Trained specialists would be able to grasp its principles and apply them autonomously. And if this new theory simultaneously yields substantial computational advantages over existing methods, the primary shift will occur in the distribution of leverage and power across human society.

This is the sole premise posited by this essay.

Let us call this scenario the **Intelligible Singularity**—in contrast to the conventional "Black Box Singularity." Its essence is that fundamental knowledge discovered by AGI returns to humanity in a compact, transparent form suitable for independent human inquiry and development.

---

### The Gray Ball: Knowledge Always Arrives with Consequences

In 2019, philosopher Nick Bostrom introduced the [Vulnerable World Hypothesis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1758-5899.12718). In his thought experiment, humanity extracts discoveries from a giant urn. White balls bring predominantly positive benefits; gray balls carry mixed consequences; a black ball, under conventional societal arrangements, renders civilizational devastation extremely likely. Bostrom notes that technologies drawn from the urn can be entangled: one breakthrough pulls a whole cluster of others into reach.

The history of science has handed us many gray balls, especially among our most powerful technologies. Nuclear physics offers a vivid case: it opened the path to atomic weapons (placing the world under the shadow of self-annihilation), while simultaneously giving us nuclear power, radiation therapy, medical imaging, and a plethora of scientific methods. Outside the laboratory walls, the trajectory of the technology was determined primarily by the political decisions of individuals and the institutional architecture of civilization as a whole.

Under our specified premise, the prospective scientific discovery of interest is this: profound mathematical knowledge that dramatically accelerates the velocity and lowers the cost of producing new results. The ramifications of such knowledge can manifest across multiple domains in a relatively short timeframe.

---

### How New Ideas Transform Scientific Capabilities

In the mid-19th century, James Clerk Maxwell unified the study of electricity and magnetism into a general theory of the electromagnetic field. His equations linked light to electromagnetic waves and predicted phenomena later confirmed experimentally. *From previously separated domains arose a coherent physical picture, from which radio communications and countless other technologies eventually grew.* [The Long Road to Maxwell's Equations](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-long-road-to-maxwells-equations).

![Homage to René Magritte's "Not to Be Reproduced" (1937)](figures/gray-wall/1_magritte_homage.jpg)

In the early 20th century, the mathematical apparatus of infinite-dimensional structures developed by David Hilbert proved, fifteen years later, to be the ideal foundation for the rigorous formulation of quantum mechanics. Then, in 1927, John von Neumann gave it its modern abstract form and applied it to quantum theory. *Thus, mathematics developed for one set of problems became the cornerstone for understanding an entirely different branch of practical science.* [Klaas Landsman's historical study](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/43513/chapter-abstract/364215973).

Computational mathematics offers another compelling precedent: the Discrete Fourier Transform. In 1965, James Cooley and John Tukey published the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm, reducing the required number of operations by orders of magnitude. The algorithm had historical precursors, but its simplicity and universality played a decisive role in digital audio, image processing, and signal analysis. *The hardware remained unchanged, but a new computational organization vastly expanded the range of tractable problems.* [MIT Course Materials on FFT](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-335j-introduction-to-numerical-methods-spring-2019/pages/week-14/).

In 2017, Ashish Vaswani and his team introduced the Transformer architecture in [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762). Here, the attention mechanism itself already existed; yet *a new structural organization of the model enabled dramatically superior machine learning outcomes at significantly reduced training time.*

Today, we are witnessing the aftermath of just this single conceptual shift as the primary catalyst pushing humanity toward a technological singularity.

Crucially, in all four historical examples, the new structure of knowledge remained entirely within the grasp of human comprehension and radically reshaped the scientific landscape. Our premise suggests that the next leap could combine these effects across multiple domains at once.

### Human-Intelligible Metamathematics of the Singularity

Algebra, geometry, analysis, combinatorics, information theory, and dynamical systems have long exchanged tools and concepts. As science advances, new correspondences between them continue to surface. Under our scenario, suppose that advanced AGI discovers a more unified, elegant mathematical language in which many of these disciplines appear as specific facets of a single relational and transformational system—without sacrificing their distinct, original content. This has repeatedly occurred in the history of science: a new theory develops its own language atop existing structures, radically compressing the descriptive complexity of multiple fields and deriving them from a unifying principle.

As early as 2010, in [How Intelligible Is Intelligence?](https://intelligence.org/files/HowIntelligible.pdf), Anna Salamon, Steve Rayhawk, and Janos Kramar explored the possibility of a compact theory of intelligence whose discovery could trigger an abrupt conceptual leap. In 2026, the authors of [Compression Is All You Need: Modeling Mathematics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20396) investigate the algorithmic compression of mathematical proofs, while [Artificial Intelligence and the Structure of Mathematics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06107) examines the global web of connections across mathematical proofs that AI may help illuminate.

On October 6, 2026, OpenAI [released a substantial corpus of mathematical results discovered with an internal AI model](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/). Analyzing and independently verifying these findings has become a massive endeavor for the mathematical community. The sheer pace and volume of such outputs raise a vital question about the next phase: will it consist of an endless proliferation of theorems and escalating complexity, or the discovery of an overarching structure that renders accumulated knowledge comprehensible and opens new avenues of inquiry accessible to human intellect?

Hence, our primary focus is a human-comprehensible mathematical theory. Such a theory could become the common heritage of science just as naturally as electromagnetic theory or functional analysis did before it—well before English became "the hottest programming language." Specialists could study it, teach it, identify errors, engineer applications, and reproduce results entirely independent of the original AI system.

In [On Scientific Understanding with Artificial Intelligence](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42254-022-00518-3), Mario Krenn and co-authors emphasize the crucial distinction between the ability to make accurate predictions and the ability to explain them. We are interested in the latter: genuine understanding of knowledge that transforms humans from passive users of magical artifacts into autonomous researchers.

In the strongest scenario, this framework would give rise to new algorithms that drastically reduce computational overhead for existing technological challenges (a nod to DeepThink).

### The Gray Wall: Capabilities Outpace Defenses

![Allusion to the owl on the cover of Nick Bostrom's "Superintelligence" (2014)](figures/gray-wall/2_bostrom_owl_homage.jpg)

The technological singularity is usually visualized as an ever-steeper exponential curve. But our hypothesized mathematical leap can generate a distinct transition dynamic. Computers are already manufactured; research teams already exist; global information networks are already functional. The moment a vastly more efficient method appears, all that existing hardware and all those teams can immediately begin tackling problems that were intractable only yesterday.

The **Gray Wall** represents the critical temporal window between the speed at which new technological capabilities emerge and the speed at which societal defenses can be deployed. Within this window, immense intellectual leverage—along with its destructive shadow—can fall into the hands of numerous independent actors long before institutions can respond. Pure mathematical insight diffuses across the globe in a matter of weeks, whereas retrofitting digital infrastructure, international treaties, regulatory frameworks, and security institutions requires months or years.

Here lies the central tension of the Intelligible Singularity: it cannot be confined inside a corporate data center. A black-box AI demands gigawatts of energy and sprawling physical infrastructure that can be monitored, guarded, and regulated. Human-intelligible mathematics, however, propagates at the speed of reading. The Intelligible Singularity democratizes the potency of new knowledge faster than society can construct safeguards.

It is entirely plausible that a few server racks, a team of talented interdisciplinary mathematicians, and specialized hardware could reconstruct a Mathematical General Intellect (MGI). While massive industrial computing will remain vital for certain empirical domains, this dynamic could expand the number of independent centers of frontier research by orders of magnitude.

To be sure, nation-states and tech giants will retain enormous advantages in infrastructure, compute scale, and capital. Yet access to a compact mathematical methodology empowers independent actors to autonomously extract insights and build capabilities currently monopolized by elite institutions.

Consider a concrete scenario within this framework: an MGI discovers an algorithm that enables rapid factorization of large numbers using modest computational resources. The resulting capabilities undermine the security of RSA—the bedrock of digital signatures and encryption protocols. Institutions must scramble to re-verify digital documents, secure connections, and cryptographic keys. Banks, cloud providers, and government agencies implement emergency protocols; international communications grow guarded; infrastructure overhauls take months. Other cryptographic paradigms have different mathematical foundations, so the impact is uneven. Nevertheless, even a localized rupture in fundamental trust mechanisms can trigger a systemic global crisis.

Now suppose this unified mathematical apparatus concurrently unlocks breakthroughs in energy, biotechnology, and materials science. The very same intellectual leverage can accelerate defensive solutions while equipping bad actors with unprecedented destructive capabilities. We are already familiar with this duality from the nuclear age; the difference lies in the number of participants and the blinding speed of diffusion. (Royalties welcome if anyone turns this scenario into a series ;)

Michael Nielsen, in his essay [ASI Existential Risk: Reconsidering Alignment as a Goal](https://michaelnotebook.com/xriskbrief/) (2025), identifies a substantial component of superintelligence risk in the sheer power it grants humans. Suppose that one primary conduit for this power is mathematical knowledge that humans can independently understand, reproduce, and extend.

### Humanity Is Already One — Now It Must Learn to Live as a Whole

Look at the screen before you. Behind your ability to read these lines stand generations of mathematicians, physicists, engineers, and workers. People extracted raw minerals, built power plants, laid undersea cables, etched microchips, and sustained global logistics. Billions of people, through their labor, built the civilization that makes reading this possible right now. Technologically, we already live as a single planetary organism.

Culturally and socially, however, we continue to operate as competing and often adversarial factions. The power of technology grows through humanity's collective labor, yet decisions regarding its deployment are made by fractured entities driven by conflicting self-interests.

Thus, the Gray Wall presents itself as a potential civilizational filter. A mathematical discovery could unlock novel energy sources, transformative healthcare, and democratized education. It could equally amplify the consequences of an error or malice by a small group to planetary scale. The survival of civilization will then hinge on how swiftly we can cultivate shared frameworks of safety, trust, and mutual responsibility—without crushing the richness of human diversity.

---

### Cultural Unity and Axial Thresholds

When our knowledge was fragmented and our technologies primitive, physical limitations served as natural buffers against our division. But when knowledge becomes open and leverage nears the absolute, the sole determining factor for survival is how human beings relate to one another. The question of whether we can perceive another as an integral part of a single shared life ceases to be an abstract moral debate. It becomes an unyielding engineering prerequisite for civilizational survival.

History has encountered epochs where compounding technological and social crises demanded an ethical transformation. Karl Jaspers characterized the "Axial Age" (800–200 BCE) as a pivotal threshold when diverse traditions simultaneously turned toward human dignity and self-reflection. [Columbia University Overview of the Axial Age](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/kpct/kp_4000bce-1000ce.htm).

In the 20th century, Jacque Fresco highlighted the structural paradox of our civilization: humanity already possesses the technological means to provide for everyone's fundamental needs, yet technology alone has not produced widespread wellbeing. The singularity could replicate this pattern: humans continue to labor, while their dependence on technologies—and the corporations that control them—only intensifies.

Today, civilizational unity has become a practical prerequisite for technological safety. By unity, I mean first and foremost the felt awareness and understanding of interconnection—whether expressed in spiritual, cultural, or scientific terms. The fundamental condition is the capacity to perceive another human being as a participant in a shared life, while preserving their autonomy and dignity.

Here, the Intelligible Singularity inevitably sheds its technical guise to reveal its core: the **Singularity of Human Relationships**.

For a civilization endowed with abruptly magnified technological leverage, this constitutes a manifestation of Robin Hanson's [Great Filter](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/greatfilter.html). Indeed, any advanced civilization likely traverses a similar threshold (we are once again not unique), repeatedly confronting the same philosophical grail of knowledge. Passing through this filter is decided not by the possession of power itself, but by the balance of human relationships when near-absolute ("divine") power becomes accessible to many. One condition stands firm: the gift of this power is viable only in conjunction with the immortality of civilization as a whole, rather than isolated individuals. Any private dreams of radical life extension or technological salvation remain an illusion if the surrounding substrate collapses: individual longevity cannot be decoupled from the collective survival of the species.

We have arrived at the threshold of near-godlike technologies. The question is whether we will learn in time to live as a whole.
